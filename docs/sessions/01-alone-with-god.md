# Alone with God
## Session 1 — Beginning, staying, and gently returning

A first practice of private prayer, grounded in Scripture. Set aside roughly 15–20 minutes if that is workable today. This is a planning aid, not a required duration. You may remain with any passage or prayer for longer, and you do not need to complete every movement.

Read this guide once before beginning. During prayer, use only the short reminder at the end, or print the guide and put your phone away. The prompts are invitations to pray in your own words; they are not prayers to recite.

### Before you begin

Choose a time and a place where you can be alone. If helpful, tell your family that you are setting aside this time and when you expect to be available. Silence notifications on your devices. Bring your Bible and a small piece of paper. Leave music off for this first attempt so you can discover whether silence suits you.

Set one reminder in your own calendar: “Time alone with God. Bring your Bible.” If you must finish at a particular time, use a single gentle end reminder; otherwise, put the clock out of sight. The intervals below are approximate and need no individual alarms.

### 1. Arrive — about 2 minutes

Sit or kneel in a position you can sustain comfortably. Let your body settle. You do not need to produce a particular feeling before beginning.

Read Matthew 6:6 slowly in your Bible.

Consider: you have made room to turn toward your Father in private. Begin by telling Him, in your own words, why you have come today. If you feel distracted or unsure how to pray, you can tell Him that too.

Put the guide down. Spend a little time simply beginning that conversation.

### 2. Worship — about 2 minutes

William Branham describes prayer as sincere communion and places worship before asking in *The Supernatural* (56-0129, paragraph 34).

Bring to mind one thing you know of God from Scripture: His mercy, faithfulness, holiness, or care. Speak to Him about it. Give thanks for one concrete kindness in your life.

Stay with that thought for a while.

### 3. Read and respond — about 5 minutes

Read Matthew 6:9–13 slowly. For today, return especially to the petition about God's will being done.

Use whichever of these questions helps you pray. Pause after each; answering all four is optional.

- **Understand:** What does this passage teach me about bringing my will before God?
- **Give thanks:** What can I thank Him for in light of what I have read?
- **Confess:** Is there something specific I need to acknowledge honestly or make right?
- **Ask:** Where do I need His help to live this out today?

Bring one real situation into this prayer: a decision, a relationship, a responsibility, or something you have been trying to control. Speak plainly. If a single thought leads into meaningful prayer, remain there and leave the other prompts aside.

### Whenever your mind wanders

This is a practice to use throughout the session, not an extra stage you must complete.

**Notice → return → continue.**

When you realize your attention has drifted, gently return to the phrase of Scripture or concern you were praying about. You may reread that phrase once and resume.

If the thought is a person or concern you want to bring before God, let it become part of your prayer. If it is a practical task you fear forgetting, write a few words on the paper, leave it for later, and return. Avoid turning the paper into a planning session.

If you remain very scattered, simplify: keep your eyes open, read one verse slowly, and respond to that one verse. Returning attention is part of learning this practice.

### 4. Pray for someone — about 2 minutes

Bring one person before God. Consider what they are facing and pray for their needs in your own words. You can also bring a concern of your own.

If confession has brought a specific wrong to mind, ask for help to put it right. A practical act of reconciliation can follow this session.

### 5. Wait quietly — about 3 minutes, or longer

In *Hear His Voice* (58-1005M, paragraph 34), Branham encourages making room to listen after praying.

Set the guide aside. Rest your attention on God and the passage you have read. You can stop filling the silence with words. If attention wanders, return gently.

If a thought seems significant, hold it thoughtfully and consider it in light of Scripture; you may write it down afterward and revisit it prayerfully.

It is also fine to finish this quiet time without a distinct impression.

### 6. Close — about 1 minute

Offer a final word of thanks in your own words. Consider one small response you can carry into today: an act of kindness, a conversation to put something right, or returning to the passage before a decision.

You may close here or keep praying. The guide does not decide when your prayer is finished.

### Afterward — a brief personal note

Once you have finished praying, consider:

- Which prompt helped me begin or return my attention?
- Where did the guide interrupt or burden me?
- What would help me protect this time tomorrow?

A sentence or two is enough. These observations help improve the guide; they are not a score for your prayer. If a planned session is missed, choose the next workable opportunity and resume.

## Keep only this nearby

**Arrive. Worship. Read Matthew 6:9–13. Respond in your own words. Pray for someone. Wait quietly.**

**When distracted: notice, return, continue.**

Stay where prayer is unfolding. The next step can wait.

## Source and adaptation notes

This session's wording, sequence, timing, attention practice, and practical arrangements are original design proposals.

- **Matthew 6:6:** private prayer. **Matthew 6:9–13:** the Lord's Prayer. Read from your own Bible.
- **William Branham, *Church Order*, 63-1226, paragraphs 84 and 92:** extended private prayer. **The Supernatural**, 56-0129, paragraph 34: sincerity and worship before asking. **Hear His Voice**, 58-1005M, paragraph 34: waiting and listening. **The Prophet Elisha**, 54-0723, paragraph 10: daily prayer and bringing decisions before God.
- **Martin Luther, *A Simple Way to Pray*, PDF, pages 7, 9–10, and 17:** freedom to remain with a thought; instruction, thanksgiving, confession, and petition. Applying the four movements to this passage is an adaptation.

For the first trial, use this same session again if useful. Familiarity may reduce the need to consult the guide. No streaks, completion targets, or prayer-duration records are required.
